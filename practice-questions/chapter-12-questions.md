## Chapter 12: Full AZ-104 Exam Simulation and Test-Taking Strategy — Practice Questions

**Q1.** You sit down for the AZ-104 exam and see it allocates 100 minutes for the exam content, with roughly 40-60 questions including a case study block near the start. Which pacing strategy BEST balances speed and accuracy across the full exam?

A) Spend an identical fixed number of seconds on every question regardless of type or complexity
B) Answer case study questions from memory of the title only, skipping the scenario text to save time for multiple choice
C) Skip the case study entirely and return to it only if time remains at the end
D) Front-load extra time to read the case study scenario once thoroughly, then divide remaining time evenly across standalone questions while flagging uncertain ones for later review

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

**Correct: D**

Case studies require upfront reading investment because the scenario contains constraints referenced by multiple questions; budgeting that time first and then dividing the rest evenly across remaining questions, with flags for uncertain ones, keeps pace realistic and preserves review time.

**Why the others are wrong:**
- Fixed identical timing per question ignores that case study questions require reading a longer scenario before answering, so it either starves the case study or overspends on simple recall questions
- Skipping the scenario text guarantees missed constraints (cost, region, compliance) that determine the correct answer, leading to wrong answers on every related question
- Deferring the case study to the end risks running out of time entirely, since it typically contains multiple linked questions worth significant exam weight

> ⚠️ Frequently missed on the exam — read the options carefully.

</details>

**Q2.** A case study on the AZ-104 exam presents a company scenario followed by 6 related questions. What is the recommended approach to reading and answering this block?

A) Read only the question stem for each of the 6 questions and search the scenario for keywords each time
B) Answer the questions in reverse order to build confidence before tackling the scenario
C) Read the entire scenario once carefully, noting constraints such as budget, region, and compliance requirements, then answer all 6 questions using that single mental model
D) Re-read the full scenario from scratch before answering each of the 6 questions

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

**Correct: C**

Reading the scenario once and extracting the key constraints up front lets you answer all related questions efficiently without repeated re-reading, which is the fastest accurate method under time pressure.

**Why the others are wrong:**
- Keyword-searching per question without full context causes you to miss constraints that only make sense when the whole scenario is understood together
- Answer order does not change the need to understand the scenario first; skipping straight to questions risks misapplying constraints
- Re-reading the entire scenario for each question wastes minutes you don't have and increases the risk of running out of time on later questions

</details>

**Q3.** Your exam has 100 minutes for 55 questions, and you've already spent 12 minutes reading and answering a 6-question case study block. Roughly how much time should you budget per remaining standalone question to stay on pace?

A) About 3.5 minutes per question
B) About 1.8 minutes per question
C) About 1.0 minute per question
D) About 6 minutes per question

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

**Correct: B**

88 minutes remain (100 - 12) for 49 remaining questions (55 - 6), which is roughly 1.8 minutes per question — enough to read, eliminate wrong answers, and flag if unsure.

**Why the others are wrong:**
- 3.5 minutes per question would only allow about 25 more questions in the remaining time, far short of the 49 that remain
- 1.0 minute per question is too aggressive and leaves large unused time, increasing pressure and risk of careless errors instead of using available time
- 6 minutes per question would allow only about 14-15 more questions, nowhere near the 49 remaining, and would cause the exam to run out of time

> ⚠️ Frequently missed on the exam — read the options carefully.

</details>

**Q4.** On a multiple-choice question you don't immediately know the answer to, two of the four options are clearly unrelated to the technology described, but you're unsure between the remaining two. What is the BEST use of the elimination technique here?

A) Guess randomly between all four options since you're unsure anyway
B) Discard the two clearly wrong options first, then apply domain-specific knowledge (cost, availability, least effort) to decide between the remaining two, and flag the question if still uncertain
C) Choose the longest of the four options, since exam writers tend to make correct answers more detailed
D) Re-read the question stem five times before deciding

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

**Correct: B**

Eliminating clearly implausible options first improves your odds from 25% to 50% on a pure guess, and applying domain cues to the remaining two maximizes the chance of picking correctly; flagging preserves the option to revisit with fresh eyes.

**Why the others are wrong:**
- Random guessing across all four options wastes the improvement gained by already spotting two wrong answers
- Answer length is not a reliable exam-writing pattern on Microsoft exams and using it as a heuristic is unfounded
- Repeated re-reading without a new strategy doesn't add information and burns time you could spend on other questions

</details>

**Q5.** A question ends with the phrase 'while minimizing administrative effort.' Which domain cue interpretation should guide your answer selection?

A) Choose the managed or automated solution that requires the least ongoing manual maintenance, even if it costs slightly more
B) Choose the cheapest possible service regardless of management overhead
C) Choose the option requiring the most manual configuration since it demonstrates the most control
D) Ignore the phrase — it is filler text with no bearing on the correct answer

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

**Correct: A**

'Minimizing administrative effort' is a standard exam cue pointing toward managed, automated, or PaaS-style solutions over manual or IaaS-heavy approaches, even when a manual option is cheaper.

**Why the others are wrong:**
- Cost alone is the wrong cue here — the phrase specifically targets management overhead, not price, and a cheaper but high-maintenance option would be wrong
- More manual configuration is the opposite of minimizing administrative effort and is a common distractor
- Exam wording like this is intentional and directly determines which option is correct; treating it as filler causes wrong answer selection

> ⚠️ Frequently missed on the exam — read the options carefully.

</details>

**Q6.** You encounter a drag-and-drop question requiring you to order five deployment steps correctly, and it's taking longer than a standard multiple-choice question. What is the BEST time-management action?

A) Abandon the question unanswered and move on immediately, since drag-and-drop questions are optional
B) Budget slightly more time for it than a typical multiple-choice question since it requires extra interaction, but flag it if you're unsure and move on once you've made a best attempt
C) Spend unlimited time on it until you are 100% certain of the correct order
D) Refuse to interact with the drag-and-drop UI and leave all items in their default order

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

**Correct: B**

Mixed-format questions like drag-and-drop and hot-area naturally take longer due to the interaction required; budgeting slightly more time while still capping your effort and flagging for review keeps overall pacing intact.

**Why the others are wrong:**
- Drag-and-drop questions are scored like any other question type — leaving them unanswered guarantees zero credit when a partial attempt might still be correct
- Unlimited time on any single question risks running out of time for the remaining exam and is never a sound pacing strategy
- Leaving items in default order without attempting the logic guarantees the question is wrong when a reasoned guess might succeed

</details>

**Q7.** With 10 minutes remaining, you have 6 questions flagged for review out of 55 total answered. What is the BEST use of the remaining time?

A) Re-answer every question from scratch, including ones you were confident about, to double-check for errors
B) Submit the exam immediately without reviewing anything to avoid overthinking
C) Spend all 10 minutes on just one flagged question until it is perfect
D) Review only the 6 flagged questions, using the remaining time to re-evaluate your reasoning and change answers only where you find a clear, specific reason to do so

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

**Correct: D**

The flag-and-review workflow exists specifically to focus limited remaining time on the questions you marked as uncertain, changing an answer only when you find a concrete reason — this maximizes the value of your last few minutes.

**Why the others are wrong:**
- Re-checking every question, including confident ones, wastes time and increases the risk of second-guessing correct answers without new information
- Submitting without any review forfeits a legitimate chance to fix genuine errors on flagged items
- Spending all remaining time on one question ignores the other five flagged questions that could also be corrected

> ⚠️ Frequently missed on the exam — read the options carefully.

</details>

**Q8.** You changed your answer on a flagged question, then start to doubt the change with 2 minutes left and no new information to justify reverting. What does exam best practice recommend?

A) Keep changing back and forth between the two answers until time runs out
B) Keep the changed answer if you had a specific, articulable reason for the change; only revert if you can identify a concrete factual error in your reasoning
C) Revert to your original answer purely because it was your first instinct, without evaluating why
D) Skip the question entirely and leave it blank to avoid the anxiety of choosing

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

**Correct: B**

Answer changes should be driven by identifiable reasoning, not anxiety; if the change was based on a specific realization, it should stand unless a concrete factual error is found — decisions should not be reversed purely from stress-based second-guessing.

**Why the others are wrong:**
- Repeatedly flipping between answers wastes remaining time and reflects anxiety-driven indecision rather than reasoned analysis
- Reverting solely because it was the 'first instinct' with no evaluation ignores that first instincts are not inherently more reliable than reasoned changes
- Leaving a question blank guarantees zero credit, whereas any attempted answer has a chance of being correct

> ⚠️ Frequently missed on the exam — read the options carefully.

</details>
